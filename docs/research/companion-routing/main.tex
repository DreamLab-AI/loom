\begin{abstract}
\noindent
A typed decision seam in an agent runtime routes each user turn to one of 115 skills whose
rubrics are all shown to the judge, plus an option to decline. Because every candidate is
exposed, an input-exposure control of the kind used to interpret grounded generation is
vacuous here: the exposed fraction is $1$, and gain over exposure is accuracy minus one,
which for our 4B local engine is $-0.116$. What this note measures instead is
\emph{gain over ranker} (\grank), accuracy minus the accuracy of the best judge-free
ranker over the same exposed options, with its decline threshold oracle-tuned on the same
corpus. That is a different instrument with different guarantees, and the two are reported
side by side so they cannot be confused. The control's first finding was a fault in itself:
two defects in the baseline, a threshold sweep that could not reach its own optimum and
exclusion clauses indexed as positive evidence, had manufactured $6.9$ of an apparent
$11.6$-point advantage. Repaired, a BM25 ranker reaches $83.7\%$ against the local engine's
$88.4\%$ (\grank${}=+4.7$, exact McNemar $p=0.455$) and a cloud judge's $93.0\%$
(${+}9.3$, $p=0.057$); against a semantic ranker at $69.8\%$ both judges win decisively.
Exact power at $n=86$ against the observed lexical effect is $0.50$. The corpus is self-authored
and single-seed, and the tuning is oracle rather than held out, so these are not deployment
estimates. The transferable findings are the repair discipline, the population breakdown,
and the use of ranker--judge disagreement as a label-audit queue, which found a corpus
error its authors had already reviewed.
\end{abstract}

\section{What this note is}\label{sec:what}
This is a companion note to a paper on exposure accounting for ontology-grounded
generation over a curated corpus.\footnote{\emph{The Copy Ceiling: An Input-Exposure
Control for Ontology-Grounded Generation over Curated Corpora}, DreamLab AI, 2026. The
routing study reported here formed a section of that paper's seventh draft and was
separated from it on 21 September 2026 following external review, because it reports a
different quantity under a name the paper had already spent.} That paper's instrument
classifies each gold item by whether the shown context exposed it and whether the answer
recovered it, and summarises the table as a scalar: the recall a verbatim copy of the shown
text would achieve, and the signed gain above it. The instrument is deterministic and
judge-free, in the tradition of input-only baselines used to deflate reading-comprehension
and inference benchmarks~\cite{kaushik2018reading,gururangan2018artifacts,poliak2018hypothesis,niven2019probing}
and of the broader argument that strong simple baselines should be run before elaborate
pipelines are believed~\cite{laitenberger2025stronger}.

Applying that instrument to a second, differently shaped task exposed a boundary in it.
On a discriminative choice over a curated option set, every candidate is shown by
construction, so the exposed fraction is $1$ and the exposure-based gain degenerates to
accuracy minus one. Something else has to play the role of the copy baseline, and what we
built is a judge-free ranker over the same exposed options. That is a legitimate and, we
argue, a necessary comparison for a seam of this kind. It is not the exposure scalar, and
the substitution was the part the original section got wrong. This note states both
quantities, reports the routing measurement against the correct one, and keeps the material
that survives contact with the corrections: two self-inflicted defects, three engines, a
population breakdown, a label audit and the reasons none of it is a deployment claim.

\section{The seam and its total exposure}\label{sec:task}
An agent runtime routes each user turn to one of 115 \emph{skills}. Each skill carries a
short rubric stating what it is for and, in 50 of the 115 cases, what it must not be chosen
for and which skill to use instead. The router puts one typed choice to a judge: the turn as
state, the 115 rubrics as options, and a \texttt{none} option for turns no skill should
handle. The gold label for a turn is the key of one exposed rubric, or \texttt{none}.

Exposure is therefore total. The gold answer is, by construction, among the text the judge
is shown, which is stronger than the generation study the parent paper reports, where
$3.6\%$ of gold lay outside the retrieved scaffold. Total exposure is exactly the condition
under which an exposure control stops discriminating: it cannot separate a judge that reads
the rubrics from one that picks at random from the same shown set.

We wrote the corpus: 86 labelled turns (16 \texttt{none}, 70 skill), each tagged by the
class of confusion it was written to probe. The corpus, the three engines and the
measurement rig are released with the runtime.\footnote{Sovereign System One, in the agent
runtime \url{https://github.com/DreamLab-AI/agentbox}: a typed-decision wire protocol
(choice, score, probability) with a local engine behind it. The router's request is
byte-identical across all three judges below, so the engine is a URL chosen by this
measurement. Decision record: ADR-2095; rig:
\texttt{crates/system-one/system-one-eval}.}

\section{Two quantities, named apart}\label{sec:naming}
Let $a$ be the seam's top-1 accuracy over the $N=86$ turns, $e$ the fraction of turns whose
gold answer appears in the shown options, and $r$ the top-1 accuracy of a specified
judge-free ranking procedure over those same options.

\begin{align}
g_{\mathrm{exp}} &= a - e \qquad\text{(gain over exposure)}\\
g_{\mathrm{rank}} &= a - r \qquad\text{(gain over ranker)}
\end{align}

On this seam $e=1$ by construction, so \gexp{} is $a-1$ and is negative for every engine
measured. That is not a defect in the engines; it is the exposure control reporting,
correctly, that it has nothing to say about a task where everything is exposed. Table~\ref{tab:quantities}
gives both numbers for the three engines so that neither can stand in for the other.

scalar and is vacuous here because every option is shown ($e=1$). \grank{} is measured
against the repaired lexical ranker ($r=0.837$) and is the quantity this note reports.}
\label{tab:quantities}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Engine & $a$ & \gexp{} & \grank{} \\
\midrule
421M encoder            & $0.535$ & $-0.465$ & $-0.302$ \\
4B cross-encoder        & $0.884$ & $-0.116$ & $+0.047$ \\
Cloud judge             & $0.930$ & $-0.070$ & $+0.093$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The distinction matters for a claim the original draft made and this note withdraws. That
draft observed that one definition of the baseline, implemented three ways, moved the
reported gain by about seven points while the classification of which rubric each turn's
gold names never moved, and read this as evidence that an exposure scalar is unstable where
its underlying counts are not. It is not evidence of that. The counts stayed fixed because
the shown options and the model's answers stayed fixed. The reported number moved because we
changed a different procedure, namely the ranker used as the baseline. What was demonstrated
is \textbf{baseline sensitivity in \grank{}}, which is a property of the instrument
introduced here, not instability in the exposure scalar of the parent paper. The general
lesson survives the correction and is worth more than the claim it replaces: when a task has
no natural no-op extractor, the baseline has to be constructed, and every construction
choice is somewhere the number can move.

\section{Constructing the ranker baseline}\label{sec:baseline}
A no-op extractor has no meaning when the answer is a key rather than a passage, so the
baseline becomes the best judge-free surface match over the exposed options: rank the 115
rubrics against the turn and take the top. We use two rankers with no trained judgement in
them: BM25 over the rubric text ($k_1{=}1.5$, $b{=}0.75$, a fixed stoplist), and cosine
similarity under a 384-dimensional sentence embedding.

A ranker has no rubric to match a decline against, so conceding \texttt{none} to the judge
would hand the judge a large and spurious advantage. Each ranker is therefore given a decline
rule whose threshold is tuned to maximise \emph{its own} accuracy on the evaluated corpus.
This is oracle tuning, and it is favourable to the baseline by design: within the chosen
ranker family, on this corpus, no threshold does better. Section~\ref{sec:caveats} states
what that does and does not buy.

\section{The control's first finding was a fault in itself}\label{sec:selffault}
Our first implementation reported a lexical baseline of $76.7\%$ ($66/86$) and a judge
advantage of $+11.6$ points. Both figures were artefacts of two defects in the baseline,
each of which inflated the judge.

\begin{enumerate}[topsep=2pt,itemsep=3pt,leftmargin=1.6em]
\item \textbf{The threshold sweep could not reach its own optimum.} A ``decline below $t$''
rule is a step function whose only breakpoints are the observed scores, but the sweep
evaluated a fixed 41-point grid spanning the observed range. The maximising window here is
$9.5715 < t \leq 9.7420$, narrower than one grid step, and the sweep stepped over it: two
\texttt{none} turns were recoverable at no cost to any correct answer and were never
recovered. Enumerating every breakpoint moves the baseline to $79.1\%$ ($68/86$) and \grank{}
to $+9.3$.
\item \textbf{Exclusion clauses were indexed as positive evidence.} Fifty of the 115 rubrics
carry a clause saying what the option is \emph{not} for and which option to use instead.
Indexed in one undifferentiated bag, those clauses are dense in exactly the vocabulary they
disclaim, so a turn about a topic scored \emph{highest} on the rubric that exists to reject
it. Dropping each clause from its marker onward moves the baseline to $83.7\%$ ($72/86$) and
\grank{} to $+4.7$.
\end{enumerate}

Together the two defects manufactured $6.9$ of the original $11.6$ points
($11.6 \rightarrow 9.3 \rightarrow 4.7$; the original draft misreported this difference as
$8.1$). In count terms the baseline moved from 66 to 68 to 72 correct of 86 while the engine
stayed at 76; Table~\ref{tab:cascade} shows the cascade.

\label{tab:cascade}

\midrule

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The second correction carries a caveat the original draft did not state. We argued there
that dropping exclusion clauses ``cannot introduce supervision'' because it uses strictly
less of the exposed text than the original procedure. Using less text is not the same as
using it neutrally. Choosing a task-aware preprocessing rule after inspecting results can
adapt a baseline to a benchmark without adding a single token, and that claim is withdrawn.
What we can defend is weaker and still worth having: the rule is stated in advance of the
numbers it produces, it is uniform across all 115 rubrics, it is motivated by a mechanism
(negative clauses are lexically dense in what they disclaim) rather than by which turns it
fixes, and it is implemented once in the released rig. Treat it as a declared preprocessing
choice, not as a neutral one, and re-declare it before any future corpus is scored.

Neither defect is a matter of taste. A baseline that indexes ``NOT for $X$'' as evidence for
$X$, or a tuning procedure that misses the maximum it claims to find, silently withdraws the
guarantee that oracle-tuning the baseline exists to buy. An instrument meant to deflate other
evaluations has to survive its own test, and ours did not until it was made to. Both defects
are fixed in the released rig with regression tests that construct each failure.

Against the embedding baseline both competent engines win decisively and the conclusion does
not depend on which of the two baselines is chosen. Against the \emph{lexical} baseline
neither does: the cloud judge's $+9.3$ points gives an exact $p$ of $0.057$ and the local
cross-encoder's $+4.7$ gives $0.455$. The two are also not distinguishable from each other
($93.0\%$ against $88.4\%$; 8 discordant turns against 4, exact $p=0.39$), so the table
separates a judge from surface matching only in the semantic case, and does not separate the
two judges from each other. Exact power at $n=86$ against the observed lexical effect is
$0.50$; roughly 157 turns would be needed for $80\%$. Observed-effect power is a weak guide,
and the operational conclusion is the pre-registration rule rather than the number: choose
the smallest difference that would change an engine decision, and size the corpus for that
before running it.

\section{Where the difference lies, and where it does not}\label{sec:decomp}
The aggregate hides the only part of this that a design choice predicted. Against the
repaired lexical baseline the 4B engine's advantage is \emph{exactly zero} on ordinary
near-neighbour picking ($86.0\%$ against $86.0\%$, $n=50$) and exactly zero on
early-discriminator turns ($83.3\%$ against $83.3\%$, $n=54$), while all of it sits on
boundary cases ($+16.7$, $n=12$) and on turns whose discriminating clause falls late in its
rubric ($+12.5$, $n=32$). That is the correspondence worth reporting: total exposure predicts
no gain on ordinary picking, and against a competent ranker one measures none.

No subgroup contrast survives Holm correction within its partition, and the two smallest
carry four discordant pairs each, where the two-sided exact $p$ floors at $0.125$ and no
effect size can reach significance. We report the pattern as a direction the design predicted,
not as a set of established contrasts. The late-discriminator tag also depends on a 48-token
cut inherited from a superseded engine, so it is a property of one configuration.

\section{Disagreement as a label audit}\label{sec:audit}
The ranker and the engine err on different turns, and their union is right on $95.3\%$
against $88.4\%$ for the engine alone. Only 26 of the 86 turns can move a paired comparison
at all: the 22 on which the procedures disagree and the 4 both get wrong. A relabelling
inside the 60 turns both get right is exactly neutral, since a wrong gold there makes both
wrong and leaves the discordant counts untouched. We therefore re-read the complete movable
set against the rubrics' own text, rather than only the turns favouring the ranker.

It found one corpus error. For \emph{``Take a screenshot of the staging login page and fill
in the demo credentials''} the corpus said \texttt{browser}, both judges said
\texttt{browser-automation}, and the \texttt{browser} rubric itself reads ``for a browser
task choose \texttt{browser-automation}, not this''. The judges followed the rubric's stated
boundary; the ranker won on vocabulary overlap. The audit is symmetric by construction
rather than by assertion, and the symmetry is testable: turn~28 is the mirror image, where
the judge's pick carries the clause ``for a visual critique of an existing UI choose
\texttt{design-audit}'' and the same criterion points \emph{away} from the judge, so the gold
was kept; turn~18 is contestable in the judge's favour and was left standing. Correcting the
one label moves \grank{} by $2.3$ points and changes no conclusion here, which is the state a
single contested label should be in.

A ranker wins by surface, a judge by the rubric's semantics, so systematic disagreement
between them localises either a mislabelled item or a genuinely ambiguous one. When gold
derives from exposed text, the judge-free baseline is therefore also a pointer at the rows to
re-adjudicate: disagreements go to an audit queue rather than being counted silently against
either procedure. This is the one claim here that needs no statistical power and that a
stronger baseline cannot take away, and the only finding in this note that survived every
correction above unchanged.

\section{Caveats}\label{sec:caveats}
\begin{itemize}[topsep=2pt,itemsep=3pt,leftmargin=1.4em]
\item \textbf{Oracle tuning is not a held-out estimate.} The decline threshold is tuned to
maximise the baseline's accuracy on the same 86 turns it is then scored on. That makes the
baseline favourable \emph{within the chosen ranker family on this corpus}, which is the
direction we want for a conservative reading of \grank{}. It does not make \grank{} a general
lower bound on what a judge adds, and it is not a deployment number. Before \grank{} is used
to justify an engine in production it needs either a held-out tuning split or a resampling
procedure that repeats the tuning inside each resample.
\item \textbf{The exclusion-clause rule is a declared choice.} See \S\ref{sec:selffault}. It
is task-aware preprocessing selected after seeing results, and the claim that it cannot
introduce supervision is withdrawn.
\item \textbf{Benchmark leakage inflates any surface baseline.} The corpus is self-authored:
86 turns, single seed, prompts written by the same hands as the rubrics they must match. Mean
content-token overlap between a turn and its own gold rubric is $37.1\%$, against $2.7\%$ for
a random rubric. The lexical baseline reported here is therefore high for a reason particular
to this benchmark.
\item \textbf{The cloud judge is non-deterministic.} It returned confidences of $0.95$,
$0.92$ and $0.93$ on identical repeated requests. Its column is a single pass and should
carry repeats before any comparison is drawn between the two competent engines.
\item \textbf{The three engines differ in training data as well as architecture}, so
Table~\ref{tab:routing} is not a controlled architecture comparison. Judge idiosyncrasy and
self-preference apply to the cloud column as they do to any model-as-judge
result~\cite{zheng2023judging,panickssery2024selfpreference}.
\item \textbf{Total exposure is not answer sufficiency.} Showing the gold rubric among 115
options is not the same as supplying the answer, and a competent choice among exposed options
can require reading two clauses against each other. The exposure family of instruments
constrains what an evaluation may attribute; it does not establish what a model did
internally~\cite{ragchecker2024,sufficientcontext2025}.
\end{itemize}

\section{What was cut during preparation}\label{sec:cut}
Four analyses were dropped rather than reported, and the reasons are part of the record.
Confidence-calibration, escalation-cascade and threshold-sweep tables were about the engine
rather than the instrument, or were structurally incapable of significance at their $n$. A
production reweighting was retracted on re-analysis: its interval was carried by two
discordant turns in a 16-item stratum resampled to hold $65\%$ of the estimand. The
threshold-sweep robustness claim survives as one sentence rather than a table, because nine
passes over the same 86 turns are one corpus re-scored nine times, the full $5.8$-point span
is five turns, and the widest pairwise contrast is itself non-significant. A cascade that
ranks cheaply and escalates only on boundary and late-clause signals is suggested by
\S\ref{sec:decomp} and is viable, but its figures were computed against the defective
baseline and must be re-derived before it is built.

\section{Reproduction}\label{sec:repro}
\texttt{system-one-eval run -{}-concurrency 1 -{}-timeout-ms 120000}, then
\texttt{system-one-eval copy-ceiling -{}-from-report \ldots{} -{}-ceilings both}, against the
released corpus at \texttt{tests/system-one/routing-cases.json}. Three operational notes cost
this preparation time and belong with the command. The local 4B engine is serialised by a
tokeniser lock, so the default concurrency queues past the timeout. Embeddings must be
requested one text per call: the estate's endpoint returns global batch offsets in
\texttt{data[].index} when concurrent requests merge. A remote backend requires a bearer
token and the rig now refuses to run without one, because an unauthenticated remote run
answers 403 on every case and is otherwise indistinguishable in the report from the backend
being unavailable.

\section{What this does and does not show}\label{sec:conclusion}
The instrument transferred to a discriminative task only by being replaced: where every
option is exposed, an exposure control is vacuous and a judge-free ranker has to take its
place. Under its own name, \grank{}, what survives is narrow and worth stating exactly.
Against a semantic ranker a competent judge wins decisively. Against a well-built lexical
ranker on this corpus it does not win measurably, at power $0.50$. The difference is located
on boundary and late-clause turns and is exactly nil on ordinary picking. The baseline's first
two implementations manufactured two-thirds of the effect they were measuring, and the
repaired control found a labelling error its authors had already reviewed.

The method claim is what generalises. A choice task whose gold is among its shown options
should report a fair judge-free baseline beside its accuracy; should tune that baseline to a
real optimum and declare every preprocessing rule that built it; should name the resulting
quantity for the baseline it is measured against rather than borrowing the name of a different
instrument; should read the gain by population rather than in aggregate; and should send the
disagreements to an audit queue rather than counting them against either procedure. None of
that requires a judge, a label budget or a model swap, which is why it is worth making
standing practice at every typed-decision seam rather than an occasional exercise.
