\section{A Second Domain: Typed Skill Routing}\label{sec:routing}
Every study above measures generation recall on one corpus. Whether the instrument transfers is a separate question, so we applied it to a task of a different shape: a discriminative choice over a curated set, where the model is shown every candidate and must name one, or decline. The first thing it measured was a fault in our own ceiling, and we report that before the result it changed.

\paragraph{Task and exposure.} An agent runtime routes each user turn to one of 115 \emph{skills}, each described by a short rubric stating what it is for and when it must not be chosen. The router puts one typed choice to a judge: the turn as state, the 115 rubrics as options, plus a \texttt{none} option for turns no skill should handle. The gold label for a turn is the key of one exposed rubric, or \texttt{none}. Exposure is therefore total: the gold answer is, by construction, among the text the judge is shown. It is stronger than in Study~1, where $3.6\%$ of gold lay outside the scaffold. We wrote the corpus: 86 labelled turns (16 \texttt{none}, 70 skill), each tagged by the class of confusion it was written to probe. The corpus, the three engines and the measurement rig are released with the runtime.\footnote{Sovereign System One, in the agent runtime \url{https://github.com/DreamLab-AI/agentbox}: a typed-decision wire protocol (choice, score, probability) with a local engine behind it. The router's request is byte-identical across all three judges below, so the engine is a URL chosen by this measurement.}

\paragraph{Adapting the ceiling to a choice task.} A no-op extractor has no meaning when the answer is a key rather than a passage, so the copy procedure becomes the best \emph{judge-free surface match} over the exposed options: rank the 115 rubrics against the turn and take the top. We use two rankers with no trained judgement in them, BM25 over the rubric text ($k_1{=}1.5$, $b{=}0.75$, a fixed stoplist) and cosine similarity under a 384-dimensional sentence embedding. A ranker has no rubric to match a decline against, so conceding \texttt{none} to the judge would hand it a large and spurious gain; each ranker is therefore given a decline rule whose threshold is tuned to maximise \emph{its own} accuracy on the evaluated corpus. The ceiling is thereby flattered and the judge's gain is a floor, provided the tuning finds the maximum it claims to. Ours did not.

\paragraph{What the instrument found first was a fault in itself.}\label{par:selffault} Our first implementation reported a lexical ceiling of $76.7\%$ and a judge advantage of $+11.6$ points. Both figures were artefacts of two defects in the control, each of which inflated the judge:

\begin{enumerate}[topsep=2pt,itemsep=2pt,leftmargin=1.6em]
\item \textbf{The threshold sweep could not find its own optimum.} A ``decline below $t$'' rule is a step function whose only breakpoints are the observed scores, but the sweep evaluated a fixed 41-point grid spanning the observed range. The maximising window here is $9.5715 < t \leq 9.7420$, narrower than one grid step, and the sweep stepped over it: two \texttt{none} turns were recoverable at no cost to any correct answer and were never recovered. Correcting the sweep to enumerate every breakpoint moves the ceiling to $79.1\%$ and the gain to $+9.3$.
\item \textbf{Exclusion clauses were indexed as positive evidence.} Fifty of the 115 rubrics carry a clause saying what the option is \emph{not} for and which option to use instead. Indexed in one undifferentiated bag, those clauses are dense in exactly the vocabulary they disclaim, so a turn about a topic scored \emph{highest} on the rubric that exists to reject it. Dropping each clause from its marker onward moves the ceiling to $83.7\%$ and the gain to $+4.7$. The operation uses strictly less of the exposure than the original, so it cannot introduce supervision.
\end{enumerate}

Together the two defects manufactured $8.1$ of the original $11.6$ points. The correction is not a matter of taste: a ceiling that indexes ``NOT for $X$'' as evidence for $X$, or a tuning procedure that misses the maximum it claims to find, silently withdraws the floor guarantee that is the whole reason for oracle-tuning the baseline in the first place. An instrument that deflates other evaluations has to survive its own test, and ours did not until it was made to.

Against the embedding ceiling both competent judges win decisively and the conclusion does not depend on the instrument chosen. Against the \emph{lexical} ceiling neither does: the cloud judge's $+9.3$ points gives an exact $p$ of $0.057$ and the local cross-encoder's $+4.7$ gives $0.455$. The two judges are also not distinguishable from each other ($93.0\%$ against $88.4\%$; 8 discordant turns against 4, exact $p=0.39$), so the table separates a judge from surface matching only in the semantic case, and does not separate the two judges from each other. Exact power at $n=86$ against the observed lexical effect is $0.50$; roughly 157 turns would be needed for $80\%$.

\paragraph{Where the difference lies, and where it does not.} The aggregate hides the only part of this that a design choice predicted. Against the repaired lexical ceiling the 4B judge's advantage is \emph{exactly zero} on ordinary near-neighbour picking ($86.0\%$ against $86.0\%$, $n=50$) and exactly zero on early-discriminator turns ($83.3\%$ against $83.3\%$, $n=54$), while all of it sits on boundary cases ($+16.7$, $n=12$) and on turns whose discriminating clause falls late in its rubric ($+12.5$, $n=32$). That is the correspondence worth reporting: total exposure predicts no gain on ordinary picking, and against a competent ranker one measures none. No subgroup contrast survives Holm correction within its partition, and the two smallest carry four discordant pairs each, where the two-sided exact $p$ floors at $0.125$ and no effect size can reach significance --- so we report the pattern as a direction the design predicted, not as a set of established contrasts.

\paragraph{Disagreement as a label audit.} The ranker and the judge err on different turns, and their union is right on $95.3\%$ against $88.4\%$ for the judge alone. Only 26 of the 86 turns can move a paired comparison at all: the 22 on which the procedures disagree and the 4 both get wrong. A relabelling inside the 60 turns both get right is exactly neutral, since a wrong gold there makes both wrong and leaves the discordant counts untouched. We therefore re-read the complete movable set against the rubrics' own text rather than only the turns favouring the ranker.

It found one corpus error. For \emph{``Take a screenshot of the staging login page and fill in the demo credentials''} the corpus said \texttt{browser}, both judges said \texttt{browser-automation}, and the \texttt{browser} rubric itself reads ``for a browser task choose \texttt{browser-automation}, not this''. The judges followed the rubric's stated boundary; the ranker won on vocabulary overlap. The audit is symmetric by construction rather than by assertion, and the symmetry is testable: turn~28 is the mirror image, where the judge's pick carries the clause ``for a visual critique of an existing UI choose \texttt{design-audit}'' and the same criterion points \emph{away} from the judge, so the gold was kept; turn~18 is contestable in the judge's favour and was left standing. Correcting the one label moves the lexical gain by $2.3$ points and changes no conclusion in this section, which is the state a single contested label should be in.

A ranker wins by surface, a judge by the rubric's semantics, so systematic disagreement between them localises either a mislabelled item or a genuinely ambiguous one. When gold derives from exposed text the copy control is therefore also a pointer at the rows to re-adjudicate --- a use the ceiling's definition does not anticipate, and the one claim here that needs no statistical power and that a stronger ceiling cannot touch. It is also the only finding in this section that survived every correction above unchanged.

\paragraph{What this does and does not show.} The instrument transferred to a discriminative task, and its first act was to find two faults in itself that had manufactured two-thirds of the effect it was measuring. What survives is narrow and worth stating exactly: against a semantic ranker a competent judge wins decisively; against a well-built lexical ranker on this corpus it does not win measurably; the difference is located on boundary and late-clause turns and is exactly nil on ordinary picking; and the control found a labelling error its authors had already reviewed. The corpus is the binding limitation: 86 turns, single seed, and prompts we wrote against rubrics we also wrote, whose mean content-token overlap with their own gold rubric is $37.1\%$ against $2.7\%$ for a random rubric. That leakage inflates any surface baseline, so the lexical ceiling reported here is high for a reason particular to this benchmark, and the gain above it is correspondingly a floor rather than an estimate. The method claim is what generalises: a choice task whose gold is among its shown options should report a fair copy ceiling beside accuracy, tune it to a real optimum, and read the gain by population rather than in aggregate.
