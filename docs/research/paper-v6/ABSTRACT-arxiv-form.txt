When a language model answers from a curated corpus via graph-based retrieval, reported grounding uplift mixes faithful delivery of facts already shown to the model with reasoning over injected structure, because gold answers derive from the same corpus. We propose the copy ceiling, the recall a verbatim copy of the shown context would achieve, and the signed gain over copy: a deterministic, judge-free, per-item control reported beside recall and paired with a seed-disjoint placebo. On a production ontology-serving node, unaided recall is 0.26 and grounded recall about 0.92, yet across ten models from eight developer families gain over copy is uniformly negative (-0.067 to -0.022). A decomposition of 11,360 gold items explains why: three unexposed items are recovered in total, context utilisation spans 0.900-0.955, and models differ only in how much exposed gold they drop. On items the scaffold does not expose, recovery falls from 0.121 bare to 0.004 grounded: injection displaces parametric recall. A paired study on the same node lifts judged quality by +0.27 pooled (p=0.002) and +0.79 where curation is deepest; a uniform-budget control shows the scaffold beats no context but not generic on-corpus text of the same length. Out of domain the interval lies inside a pre-specified margin. A two-edge composition test separates models that compose exposed structure from those that do not. Applied to a second domain, typed skill routing over 115 shown rubrics, a fair ceiling locates a 4B cross-encoder's gain in declining and late-clause discrimination (+25.0, +18.8 points) while picking among exposed options gains +4.0, and ranker-judge disagreement doubles as a label audit. Evaluations whose gold derives from the injected corpus should report a copy ceiling as standard.
